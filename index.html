No. 02

What Matters Now

AI made coding fast. Tickets still wait in review, testing and sign-off. I work on everything around the model: the process and the tooling. If a step doesn't need a human decision, I want it automated. Engineers' attention is the expensive part.

  • A delivery process built for AI

    The harness around the model is as important as the model itself: checks in the pipeline, automated tests, review effort that matches the risk, and people accountable for what ships. The goal is faster delivery without more broken releases. It is never finished. Models and tools change every few months, and the harness has to change with them.

    Measured with streamline

  • A toolkit the whole team improves

    When every engineer writes their own prompts and commands, each one repeats the same work. A shared kit holds the team's standards, a map of which service owns what, and skills for review, testing and documentation. It changes through merge requests like any other code. One person's fix becomes everyone's.

    Cursor rules · skills · agents · MCP

  • Choosing what to build

    When a prototype takes an afternoon, the scarce skill is knowing which one deserves a second day. I pull engineers into discovery and customer data from the start, so "is this the right problem?" gets asked before the first commit.

    Tried with my own team: the Product Engineer model

  • AI that says when it doesn't know

    Most AI products always produce an answer, because output feels like value. In Vinn, the engine is built to stop and say what is missing when it doesn't have enough context. Checking and limiting what a model is allowed to claim is a big part of the engineering now.

Vinn

A plan for when the decision is someone else's.

A promotion, a budget approval, a deal with a landlord: outcomes you can influence but can't decide alone. You describe what you want and who has a say. Vinn lays out the steps, how you will know each one worked, and how each one could fail. When something new happens, it updates the plan.

Why not just ask a chatbot?
  • It remembers the people. What you learn about your manager or a landlord carries over to the next time you deal with them.
  • It says when it doesn't know enough. It tells you what to find out first, and it won't make up a plan.
  • The whole plan fits on one board. You can read it at a glance, without scrolling back through a long chat.

No. 05

What Guides Me

1

Decisions sit next to the work

A team can only decide well if it sees what I see: business metrics, customer feedback, constraints, budget. I share as much of it as I'm allowed to, give each area one owner, and leave the call with them. Making real decisions, and owning the result when one goes wrong, is also how engineers grow fastest. My job is to make sure they have the context, and to stand behind them when they need it.

2

Outcomes are the metric

I check whether commitments are met and goals are reached. Where people work, how many hours they log and how many story points they burn tell me very little, and velocity turns into a game the moment it becomes the target.

3

Loosely coupled, independently deployable

Kafka and clean domain boundaries aren't the goal, they're how a team ships on Tuesday without waiting on three others. I architect for independence first, because that's what lets people move fast safely. Even more so now that code arrives faster than anyone can read it.

4

Dissent needs a strategy

Rebel is in the name, so here is the caveat. Reading Rebels at Work, I recognised myself as the book's bad rebel: plenty of energy and ideas, pushed without a plan, alienating the people I needed. Now I frame proposals around what the organisation already wants, find allies first and pick my moment. Sometimes the right move is not to push at all.
